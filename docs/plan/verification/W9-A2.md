## 3. Findings ARGUED DOWN, with the argument

### W9-04 5.4 — the uniqueness assertion is redundant, and the prescribed fix does not change that

The report is right that `const words = addresses.map(([, label]) => label)` reads the test's own
answer key: no product edit can redden it. **But its prescribed repair — build the word list from
`topCrumbs(...)` instead — does not make it reachable either**, and that is worth writing down
because it would otherwise be filed again next wave.

The nine rows above it already assert, per address, that `topCrumbs` returns exactly the expected
label. Whenever those nine pass, the product's answers *are* the nine literals, so uniqueness over
the product is uniqueness over the same nine strings; and whenever any of them fails, it throws
first. The two forms are equivalent for every input. The only edit that can redden either is an
author typing one word twice in the literal — which was the report's own diagnosis of the original.

The repair was applied anyway (the derived form documents where the answer comes from, and costs
nothing), **but it is recorded here as a clarity change and not as a closure.** What actually turns
this block into a gate is deriving the *address* list from the route table, which is the case beside
it — W9-04/5.3 above. One roster read off the registry closes what two hand-written rosters could
not.

## 8. Should this mechanism outlive the plan?

**Yes — and the partition has to change with it.**

The eight-wave record now reads: builders disclose about 11–20% of their own holes under six waves of
instruction, verifiers find them, and a closer converts filed findings at a rate nothing else comes
near. Wave 8 measured 17 of 17 and said the scarce resource was *filed findings*. This wave doubled
the input — forty rows against twenty-three, because house rule 18's amendment made the file the
first act of a pass rather than the last — and the scarce resource moved: **28 of 40 rows' closing
cases live in files the closer is forbidden to touch.** One gate script holds ten of them.

Three things are worth carrying, in order of what they cost to learn:

1. **House rule 18's amendment is the highest-yield rule in this plan, and it is not adversarial.**
   Seven of ten passes died silently in wave 8; five of five filed this wave. The file-first rule
   nearly doubled the closer's input for no extra work.
2. **The closer needs the test trees, all of them, including the gate scripts' fixture sections and
   the browser tree.** Not the production sources — handing those back is right and wave 8 was right
   to do it six times. But a fixture inside `scripts/feature-matrix-check.sh` and a
   `page.waitForRequest` inside `frontend/e2e/` are *cases*, and a partition that keeps them with the
   packet that just proved it could not see the hole is a partition that costs a wave per finding.
   Ten of this wave's twenty-eight hand-backs are one file.
3. **House rule 13 needs a corollary.** *A verifier's finding is an owned rule or a case, never a
   paragraph* — but six of this wave's rows need **one word of production visibility** before any case
   can exist: `private` → `private[app]`, one `mvnDeps` line, one refusal. A finding whose case is
   blocked on a one-word seam is a paragraph until somebody is allowed to type that word. Either the
   closer may make a *visibility-only* edit, or every verification pass must name the seam as a
   separate owned row so the file's owner lands it before it freezes.

And the thing the eight waves have proved that needs no change: **naming one ungated rule per packet
and making its owner close it works — forty of forty across four waves.** The adversaries exist to
find the other four and a half per packet.
